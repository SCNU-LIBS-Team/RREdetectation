In this work, an automatic curve fitting method based on a continuous-wavelet transform (CWT) is proposed to
resolve overlapped peaks and to adaptively extract the major peaks in laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy
(LIBS). From the local minimum of the second derivative of the LIBS spectrum calculated with CWT, the number
of individual peaks is determined, and corresponding peak positions are estimated. The full width at half-
maximums (FWHMs) of individual peaks are estimated from the separation of two maxima siding the minimum.
A threshold is introduced to eliminate the small peaks and therefore reduce the number of fitting parameters and
adaptively extract the major peaks with different spectral intensities. The Trust-Region algorithm is used for
parameter optimization. The proposed method is used to analyze both simulated LIBS spectra and experimental
overlapped peaks. Both simulated and experimental results show that the proposed method can resolve over-
lapped peaks even with a low separation degree, although the minimum resolvable separation degree depends
on the FWHM ratio and strength ratio of individual peaks and the wavelet scale. In a LIBS calibration experiment
of N2∕SF6 gasses mixture, after resolving the overlapped peaks with the proposed method, better linear corre-
lations between the concentration and intensity of F (with an adjusted R-squared value 0.9972), as well as between
the concentration ratio and intensity ratio of nitrogen to fluorine (with adjusted R-squared values >0.98 and
0.99) are obtained. © 2018 Optical Society of America

1. INTRODUCTION
Laser-induced breakdown spectroscopy (LIBS) is widely used in
many fields such as material analysis [1], environmental mon-
itoring [2], biomedicine [3], archaeology [4], food safety [5],
and so on. In principle, LIBS can analyze any matter, regardless
of its physical state, be it solid [6], liquid [7] or gas [8], and it can
detect all elements. However, the accuracy and reliability of the
analysis are affected by peak overlapping, which commonly
occurs in LIBS spectra due to various line-broadening mecha-
nisms and the limitation of instrumental resolution. Besides, in
many cases, the spectral analysis is performed manually and is
therefore time-consuming, which makes online and real-time
applications of LIBS somewhat difficult.
In the past, signal processing methods such as the iterative
deconvolution method [9], Wiener filtering [10], and the curve
fitting method [11–14] were widely used to resolve overlapped
peaks in LIBS spectra. However, the effectiveness of iterative
deconvolution and Wiener filtering is influenced by the estima-
tion accuracy of the broadening function. In addition, Wiener
filtering also requires information on noise, such as the signal-
to-noise ratio and power spectrum. On the other hand, the
curve fitting method is more effective and is therefore the most
commonly used approach in resolving overlapped peaks.
Many researchers have focused on improving the accuracy of
curve fitting. For example, Zhang et al. [12] proposed an initial
estimation method in which appropriate initial values were ob-
tained by applying a fractional differential theory. Tan et al.
[13] proposed an error compensation method to achieve reso-
lution and correction of overlapping peaks. Using an iterative
procedure, the fitting residual was fed back to the overlapping
peaks, and multiple curve fitting processes were performed to
obtain a solution with a lower residual. Furthermore, an auto-
matic modeling algorithm was also investigated for spectra data
processing. Alsmeyer and Marquardt [14] proposed an iterative

automatic modeling algorithm. The shortcoming of these
methods was the requirement of a large amount of calculation
or iteration.
In this work, an automatic curve fitting method is proposed
to resolve the strongly overlapped peaks that could not be re-
solved by a spectrometer and to achieve a better resolution for
the spectrometer-measured LIBS spectral data. In this proposed
method, the second derivative of the LIBS spectrum is calcu-
lated by a continuous-wavelet transform (CWT). The number
and positions of individual peaks of the LIBS spectrum are
obtained from the local minimum of the second derivative.
The full width at half-maximums (FWHMs) of individual
peaks are estimated from the separation of the two maxima sid-
ing the local minimum. In addition, a threshold is introduced
to eliminate small peaks in the LIBS data and to adaptively
extract the major peaks with different spectral intensities.
Also in the proposed method, a Trust-Region algorithm is em-
ployed to minimize the deviation between the mathematical
model and the experimental LIBS spectrum. The effectiveness
of the proposed method is demonstrated with both simulated
and experimental LIBS spectra with overlapped peaks.
2. THEORY
Usually, in a curve fitting method to resolve overlapped peaks
in LIBS spectra, there are three key factors: (1) an appropriate
mathematical model to correctly describe the overlapped peaks
in LIBS spectra. The line shapes of the LIBS peaks depend
heavily on the line-broadening mechanisms in laser-induced
plasma. The Lorentzian shape is commonly used when Stark
broadening is dominant, while the Voigt shape is more accurate
but requires additional fitting parameters; (2) an appropriate
optimization method. The selected optimization method
should have good convergence and global searching capabil-
ities; (3) reasonable initial values for fitting parameters in
the multi-parameter optimization. Appropriate initial fitting
values can contribute to a faster convergence speed, a better
fitting precision, a higher computation efficiency, and a shorter
running time.

In this work, a simple method to avoid the interference of
artifacts is proposed. As shown in Fig. 1, for a real peak there is
always one maximum on each side of the minimum, and the
interference of artifacts can therefore be eliminated by checking
the two maxima on both sides of the minimum. Due to arti-

and last paired maximum/minimum need to be checked. The
first minimum must be excluded if the first maximum is located
on the right of the first minimum. Similarly, the last minimum
must be excluded if the last maximum is located on the left of
the last minimum. As shown in Fig. 2, the proposed algorithm
can effectively avoid the interference of artifacts and improve
peak detection accuracy.
D. FWHMs and Heights of Individual Peaks
To effectively determine FWHMs of the individual peaks, the
relationship between FWHM and Δx (as shown in Fig. 1, Δx is
defined as the separation of two maxima on both sides of the

pends slightly on the wavelet scale value. FWHM can therefore
be estimated roughly from the fitted linear relationship pre-
sented in Fig. 3, which is obtained by a least square method.

E. Optimization Algorithms
The key steps of the proposed automatic curve fitting method
are described as follows:
1. Select an appropriate wavelet scale according to the
signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) and perform CWT on the original
signal to obtain the second derivative. A smaller scale can obtain
a higher resolution for peak detection, but it is more sensitive to
noise. A larger scale can achieve a better noise suppression
effect, but the resolution for peak detection will be reduced.

2. Set a threshold and extract only individual peaks in
which the corresponding minimum values are greater than
that threshold. This threshold is introduced for adaptively
extracting the major peaks with different spectral intensities
in the LIBS spectrum. Those peaks that satisfy the condition
jyj ≥ ξ · jyjmax will be extracted, where ξ is the threshold, and
jyj is the absolute value of the local minimum of the second
derivative.
3. Determine the FWHMs of individual peaks from the
separation of the two maxima on both sides of the minimum.
As FWHMs are strongly influenced by Stark broadening,
which depends on energy level, particle species, and ionization
state, all FWHMs are fitted independently in our algorithm.
4. Determine the heights of the individual peaks.
5. Minimize Eq. (3) using the Trust-Region algorithm to
determine simultaneously the parameters of the individual
peaks.
6. Calculate the LIBS spectrum and compare to the
measurements.
In the proposed curve fitting method, all calculations are
performed with a program written in MATLAB. A threshold
of 0.01 can satisfy most cases. The scale a is related to the in-
strumental resolution; after the instrument is selected, the scale
a can be pre-set and will not change in most cases. On the other
hand, parameters such as number, positions, FWHMs, and
heights of the individual peaks can be estimated automatically
by the proposed method. From this sense, the proposed curve
fitting method can be realized automatically to analyze the
LIBS spectrum.
3. SIMULATION AND EXPERIMENT
Several LIBS spectra with overlapped peaks of different param-
eters (separation degree, peak height, and FWHM) are simu-
lated and used to verify the feasibility of the proposed curve
fitting method for resolving overlapped peaks. Each simulated
spectrum is composed of two noise-free Voigt peaks. The sep-
aration degree is defined by κ  jλ0,1 − λ0,2j∕2γ1  γ2, and
λ0 and γ are the peak position and FWHM of individual peaks,
respectively [12]. A wavelength interval of 0.05 nm is chosen
according to the spectral resolution of the spectrometer used in
our experiment.
In addition, the proposed curve fitting method is also
applied to resolve individual peaks in experimental LIBS spec-
tra. The LIBS experimental setup is described as follows.
A Q-switched 1064 nm Nd:YAG laser (Continuum Surelite,
with maximum pulse energy 700 mJ, pulse width 18 ns,
and repetition rate 1–10 Hz) is focused into gas samples (com-
positions of N2∕SF6 gas mixtures are listed in Table 1) by a
focusing lens with a 50 mm focal length. The plasma emission
is detected and recorded by a HORIBA iHR320 JY spectrom-
eter (equipped with a 1200 grooves/mm grating and the cor-
responding wavelength interval is 0.052 nm) equipped with an
intensified charge-coupled device (ICCD). For LIBS spectrum
measurements, the laser pulse energy is 50 mJ and the repeti-
tion rate is 5 Hz. The slit width of the spectrometer is set to
50 μm. The wavelength of the spectrometer is calibrated by a
mercury lamp, and its spectral response is calibrated by a
tungsten lamp and a deuterium lamp. Instrumental line-
broadening is measured by a single-frequency He–Ne laser (with
center wavelength 632.8 nm and FWHM 0.29 pm). With
50 μm slit width, the instrumental broadening shows a
Lorentzian profile with a FWHM of 0.1367 nm, and its effect
on the measured LIBS spectrum is considered in the data
processing.

Figure 5 shows how spectral parameters such as strength ra-

shown in Fig. 5(a), the peak height (intensity) ratio I1∕I2 also

relationship between κm and the scale a. The result indicates
that a smaller scale a contributes to a better resolving perfor-

scale. In the case that the spectrometer resolution is 0.05 nm

5. CONCLUSIONS
In conclusion, a CWT-based automatic curve fitting method
was proposed for resolving severely overlapped peaks in the
LIBS spectrum. CWT was employed to calculate the second
derivative of the LIBS spectrum, from which the number, po-
sitions, and FWHM linewidths of individual peaks were deter-
mined and used as initial parameter values in multi-parameter
optimization to accurately extract the spectral parameters of
major individual peaks in the LIBS data. Experimentally, for
LIBS analysis of a N2∕SF6 gas mixture, the extracted spectral
parameters from severely overlapping peaks were used to estab-
lish a calibration curve between F concentration and a LIBS
intensity of F 730.901 nm line with a better linear fit (with
adjusted R-squared value improved from 0.8731 to 0.9972),
as well as between the N/F concentration ratio and intensity
ratio of the corresponding nitrogen line to fluorine F
823.077 nm line (with adjusted R-squared value improved
to be >0.98 and 0.99) for quantitative composition analysis.
Furthermore, even better performance is expected with more
seriously overlapped peaks. The proposed automatic curve fit-
ting method has great potential for online and real-time appli-
cation of LIBS, as well as for other spectral signal processing.
